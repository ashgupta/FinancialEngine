1. The idea (Cem Karsan's point)

A stock price is just the average outcome. The option chain shows the whole range of outcomes the market is pricing — how likely a −30% crash is, how likely a +30% jump is. Two stocks with the same price can have completely different shapes underneath, and that shape tells you whether to hedge with puts, speculate with calls, or just own the stock.

4. How it's calculated (do it yourself)

  1. Pick one expiry (here Mar 19, 2027) and pull every strike's bid/ask for calls and puts.
  2. Use out-of-the-money options (puts below the price, calls above) at the bid-ask mid; skip strikes with no bid or very wide quotes.
  3. Find the forward price from put-call parity near the money: F = K + erT(Call − Put). This automatically accounts for dividends.
  4. Convert each price to implied volatility and fit a smooth curve through them (the "smile"). Smoothing is essential; raw prices are too noisy.
  5. Breeden–Litzenberger (1978): the probability density at strike K is the second derivative of the call price with respect to strike: f(K) = erT · ∂²C/∂K². Intuitively, a tight butterfly centred at K costs roughly the probability of finishing near K.
  6. Integrate the density to get tail probabilities and percentiles; compare with a lognormal at the ATM vol to see where the market is paying extra.

Without coding: thinkorswim → Analyze → Probability Analysis; Interactive Brokers → Probability Lab; OptionStrat or Market Chameleon "implied probability" pages; or compare put vs call implied vol at equal distance from the price (put IV much higher = left fat tail).

5. Using it (Karsan's two examples)

  • Left fat tail (LLY, NVO, VRTX): if you hold the stock, the risk you most need to cover is the one the market is pricing. A 4–6 month ~85–90% put or put spread caps it. If you're buying, selling a put is paid well for that tail, but only do it if you'd happily own it lower.
  • Lottery shape (MRNA): if bullish, an out-of-the-money call puts ~5–10% of the capital at risk for the right-tail payoff, leaving the rest for diversification. Owning the stock means owning the 30% chance of a −30% drop too.
  • Narrow shape (PFE): options add little; the stock is the simpler instrument.
Caveats: these are risk-neutral probabilities — prices, not forecasts. They include a risk premium (investors overpay for crash protection), so real-world crash odds are usually lower than the left tail shows. Tails beyond the last traded strikes are extrapolated (flat smile). Results depend on quote quality; NVO/PFE have fewer strikes. Educational only, not investment advice.
